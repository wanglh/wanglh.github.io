除了发现深度伪造：教导多语言学习者质疑人工智能

原文标题: Beyond spotting deepfakes: Teaching multilingual learners to interrogate AI

Key points:

  • When it comes to AI, visual access must be paired with visual questioning

  • 5 ways AI can strengthen your teaching this school year

  • Schools are building AI rules before they know the destination

  • For more on AI in the classroom, visit eSN’sDigital Learninghub

Long before computers entered our homes and classrooms, photographs helped shape what people believed. Images carried a particular kind of authority: We did not simply look at them; we often treated them as evidence. They helped audiences understand events they had never witnessed themselves and gave distant stories a sense of immediacy and truth.

Yet images have never been neutral witnesses. They can be selected, cropped, staged, retouched, captioned, or removed from their original context. Photographic manipulation predates the digital age. What artificial intelligence has changed is not the possibility of visual deception, but its speed, accessibility, scale, and increasingly convincing realism.

Today, students can encounter an AI-generated photograph, a cloned voice, or a convincing video of an event that never occurred—all while scrolling through the same feed where they see authentic news and messages from friends.

For multilingual learners, this deserves particular attention. Visuals often bridge linguistic gaps, making concepts accessible before students have the language to fully explain them.

But when images can be generated, manipulated, or taken out of context, visual access must be paired with visual questioning.

This is not because multilingual learners are inherently more susceptible to misinformation.

Rather, if we use visuals as a bridge to understanding, we must also teach students to question their source, context, and credibility.

From looking to investigating

One response to deepfakes is to teach students how to spot them: Look for distorted hands, unusual shadows, unnatural facial movements, or inconsistencies in the background.

These clues may help, but they have a limitation: AI is improving. The visual flaw that exposes an AI-generated image today may disappear tomorrow.

Students therefore need to move beyond asking:

“Does this look real?” and learn to ask: “What evidence do I have that this is real?”

Research from Stanford’sCivic Online Reasoninginitiative found that students often struggled to judge the credibility of online information, challenging the assumption that growing up with technology makes young people skilled evaluators. Professionalfact-checkers used a different strategy: lateral reading—leaving a questionable page to investigate its source and seek independent evidence.

Applied to deepfakes, the lesson is simple: Don’t just look more closely at the image.

Look beyond it.

For multilingual learners, teachers can make both the thinking and language of verification explicit: What do I see? What is the claim? Who created it? Where else can I check? What evidence do I have? Students can discuss their reasoning with a partner or in their strongest language before communicating conclusions in English, supported by frames such as This source claims…, I questioned this because…, and The evidence does/does not support…

Google Arts & Culture’s Odd One Out, which asks students to identify an AI generated image among authentic artworks, offers a low-language-entry way to practise this reasoning.

Teachers can move beyond simply spotting the “fake” by asking students what made them question the image, what evidence supports their judgment, and where else they could check.

Interpret, generate, evaluate

Questioning AI-generated content is only part of AI literacy. Students also need opportunities, with appropriate safeguards, to understand how it is constructed. A useful progression is to interpret, generate, and evaluate.

Interpret: Students examine an image or video, considering its source, framing, context, purpose, and audience. For multilingual learners, visuals provide an accessible entry point, while structured discussion develops the language needed to express increasingly complex interpretations.

Generate: Students might create two AI-generated images of the same fictional event, changing the perspective, wording, or framing of the prompt. They then compare what changed, what was emphasised or omitted, and which version appears more convincing.

The goal is not simply to learn prompting, but to experience how choices shape the story an image appears to tell.

Evaluate: Students return to the evidence: Who created this? What supports the claim? What do reliable sources say? What might be missing? What would make me reconsider my conclusion? In doing so, they use academic language to question, corroborate, challenge, justify, and revise.

This progression reflects higher-order processes in Bloom’s Revised Taxonomy: students analyse how visual meaning is constructed, apply their understanding through creation, and evaluate claims using evidence, context, and credibility.

Linguistic scaffolding does not lower expectations; it gives multilingual learners greater access to these sophisticated cognitive processes.

Beyond detecting deepfakes

These skills extend beyond AI-generated content. A genuine photograph paired with a false caption can mislead. An authentic video removed from its original context can distort an event. The larger goal is therefore not simply to teach multilingual learners how to detect AI. It is to give them the tools and language to evaluate claims and evidence.

Stanford’s research offers an encouraging message: these skills can be taught. Studies evaluating Civic Online Reasoning instruction have shown that students can improve their ability to judge the credibility of online information.

Importantly, we do not want students to leave our classrooms believing that nothing can be trusted. Skepticism without the skills to investigate can quickly become cynicism.

The goal is not: “Everything could be fake.”

It is: “I don’t know whether I should trust this yet. Let me find out.”

Protection through preparation

Schools have legitimate reasons to establish boundaries around AI, particularly around privacy, safeguarding, age, and assessment. But boundaries need to be accompanied by education.

This is especially important for multilingual learners. We have long recognised the power of visuals to make learning more accessible. In the age of generative AI, we need to extend that principle: access to visual information must be accompanied by the tools to interrogate it.

Keeping AI outside the classroom will not keep it outside students’ lives.

We do students a disservice if they leave school knowing how to avoid AI but not how to question an AI-generated image, investigate its source, corroborate its claims, or decide whether it should be shared.

Our responsibility is to give every learner—including those still developing the language of instruction—the tools and language to interpret what they see, generate responsibly, investigate what they encounter, and evaluate what deserves their trust.

Because in the age of deepfakes, perhaps the most important question we can teach a student to ask is not: “Is this real?” but: “How do I know?”

为了重建成人学习者的信心，大学必须首先赢得他们的信任

原文标题: To rebuild adult learner confidence, universities must first earn their trust
来源: eCampusNews | 发布时间: 2026-08-24
原文链接: 点击阅读原文

Key points:

  • Adult learners want institutions that help them choose a strategic path forward

  • Most states struggle to bring adult learners back to college

  • Higher ed faces mounting challenges amid evolving expectations

  • For more news on adult learners, visit eCN’sCampus Leadershiphub

First-time college enrollment among adults declinednearly 16 percentin fall 2025, ending a four-year streak of post-pandemic growth.

The decline comes as colleges and universities are already facing enrollment pressure fromfewer high school studentspursuing four-year degrees, making adult learners an increasingly important population for sustaining enrollment.

It’s not that adults have stopped valuing higher education. Rather, they’re making enrollment decisions in a very different environment than they were even a few years ago. AI is reshaping the workforce, career paths are rapidly evolving, and the long-term value of various credentials feels harder to predict.

This presents an opportunity for universities to rethink how they engage prospective adult learners. In a moment defined by uncertainty, institutions can help students navigate their options with greater clarity, transparency, and support.

Why adult learners are hitting pause

Part of the enrollment decline reflects a natural post-pandemic correction. In the years immediately following COVID-19, more adults returned to school after experiencing layoffs, career disruptions, and shifts in the job market. As that wave of adult learners levels off, institutions are no longer seeing the same enrollment momentum.

At the same time, AI is transforming industries, making it harder for professionals to predict which skills will remain valuable and which educational path will best position them for long-term success. Rather than moving straight into enrollment, many adults are taking a more deliberate approach.

The growing number of certificates, bootcamps, employer-sponsored training programs, and other alternative credentials has only made the decision more complex. With an abundance of options, prospective students face greater pressure to determine which educational investment will deliver the greatest return.

Even the motivations that inspire adults to return to school can become the reasons they hesitate. A professional seeking a promotion may worry about balancing coursework with a full-time job. A parent hoping to improve their family’s financial future may question whether the upfront cost is worth it. Someone looking to transition into a growing field may struggle to identify which credential will open the right doors.

Together, these challenges underscore that the question is no longer whether higher education is worthwhile — it’s whether a particular pathway is the right investment at this moment in a student’s career.

Rather than focusing solely on persuading more adults to enroll, universities should focus on supporting informed decisions. By helping prospective students connect their educational goals to meaningful career outcomes, institutions can become trusted partners in one of the biggest decisions adult learners will make.

3 ways to rebuild confidence among adult learners

Universities can build prospective students’ confidence by reducing uncertainty at every stage of the enrollment journey. Here are three ways to do it.

  1. Create a clear path from investment to opportunity

Now more than ever, adult learners want to understand how a program will help them achieve their goals and whether it’s worth the investment.

Universities should be transparent upfront about what matters most: the credential students will earn, the cost and duration of the program, the skills they’ll gain, and the career opportunities those skills can unlock.

For example, clearly outline how a healthcare management program prepares graduates for leadership roles by cultivating strategic decision-making and team management skills. Or highlight how a nine-month data analytics certificate can also award credit toward a future master’s degree, giving students the flexibility to continue their education when the time is right.

By helping prospective students understand how a program can lead to tangible career outcomes, institutions make it easier for them to move forward. In turn, institutions become trusted partners in helping students achieve their career aspirations, not just a place to earn a credential.

  1. Lower the stakes of taking the first step

For many adults, returning to school can feel like an all-or-nothing decision. Universities can make taking the first step more appealing by creating pathways that reduce the perceived risk of enrolling.

Stackable certificates that count toward a future degree are one example. Rather than asking students to commit to a multi-year program, institutions can allow them to earn a credential with immediate workforce value while keeping the door open for continued education.

Flexible scheduling, online learning options, and multiple entry points can provide similar reassurance by making it easier to balance education alongside work and family responsibilities.

When adult learners can build momentum without feeling like they’re putting their lives on hold, taking the first step feels far less daunting. That flexibility empowers prospective students to begin their educational journey on terms that fit their lives.

  1. Make the enrollment journey more personal

Sometimes, questions about cost or scheduling can mask deeper concerns about belonging or the ability to succeed after years away from the classroom.

That’s why outreach should go beyond promoting programs. Dedicated enrollment specialists, personalized guidance, and opportunities to ask questions of existing students, alumni, and faculty can help adult learners navigate uncertainty before they commit.

Whether it’s explaining transfer credits, mapping out a realistic course schedule, or discussing work and family responsibilities, these conversations show prospective students that the institution is invested in their success from the very beginning. That reassurance can make the difference between hesitation and feeling confident enough to move forward.

Meet adult learners where they are

Higher education hasn’t become less relevant for adult learners. If anything, it’s become more important as careers, industries, and workforce expectations continue to evolve.

But adult learners aren’t looking for institutions that simply promise opportunity. Instead, they’re looking for ones that help them choose a strategic path forward.

Universities that take on this role can build trust with students long before they enter the classroom. And that trust is what helps institutions attract, enroll, and retain the adult learners shaping the future of higher education.

金融教育不得牺牲学生隐私

金融教育不得牺牲学生隐私

原文标题: Financial education must not sacrifice student privacy
来源: eSchoolNews | 发布时间: 2026-08-24

Key points:

  • Most teachers are not trained as privacy officers

  • We need accessible data and greater student agency

  • Data-driven decision-making is the key to unlocking K-12 district success

  • For more news on financial education, visit eSN’sEducational Leadershiphub

Financial education is gaining real momentum across the United States. More states are adopting mandates, more schools are adding graduation requirements, and more policymakers are acknowledging that students need practical preparation for the financial decisions they will face as adults.

That progress matters because every student will eventually make consequential choices involving money, including renting a home, paying for higher education, starting a job, using credit, buying insurance, and planning for long-term financial security. Strong financial education can help young people approach those decisions with greater knowledge and confidence.

Recent analysis from the National Financial Educators Council, however, identified a serious gap in how some states are implementing that education. In ten states and Washington, D.C., financial education standards or legislation direct students to complete classroom activities involving documents that can require personally identifiable information, including Social Security numbers, birthdates, home addresses and other sensitive details.

The documents include W-4s, financial-aid applications, loan forms and banking paperwork. Students should understand how these forms work, but standards that call for their completion without explicitly requiring sample data or protected classroom versions create an unnecessary risk that real personal information could be entered, collected, or stored.

The problem is not the subject matter. It is the absence of safeguards.

Students can learn the purpose, structure, and use of financial documents without entering real personal information. Classroom versions should remove, redact, or block sensitive fields rather than rely solely on instructions telling students to leave them blank or provide fictional information. Directions can be misunderstood or overlooked, while a well-designed form prevents sensitive information from being requested in the first place.

There is no educational justification for exposing a minor’s identity data. A Social Security number combined with a name, birthdate and home address can provide much of what an identity thief needs to damage a child’s credit and financial future. Because minors rarely monitor their credit histories, the harm may stay hidden for years, surfacing only when they apply for financial aid, seek employment, rent an apartment or open their first credit account.

In the states examined, the relevant standards and legislation do not clearly require redacted forms, educator privacy training, secure handling procedures or protocols for storing, transmitting and disposing of sensitive information. That omission creates a preventable student privacy risk and points to a larger problem in how financial education policy is sometimes designed and implemented.

Classroom realities compound the danger. Once personal information is written on a form or entered into a digital document, a series of practical questions follows. Will the assignment be collected, graded, or stored? Could it be uploaded to a learning management system, sent by email, printed, left on a desk, or discarded in an ordinary trash can? Will a third-party platform retain any of the information?

Teachers should not be expected to answer those questions on their own. Most are not trained as privacy officers, compliance specialists, cybersecurity professionals or records-management experts. Asking them to handle sensitive student information without clear guidance creates risks not only for students and families but also for educators, schools and districts.

Poorly designed requirements could also undermine public confidence in financial education itself. Advocates have spent decades working to establish the subject as a serious part of K-12 education. If its expansion produces avoidable privacy failures, the resulting loss of trust could weaken support for programs that students genuinely need.

The deeper issue is policy design. Financial education is too often treated as a simple addition to graduation requirements rather than as an academic subject requiring rigorous standards, qualified educators, strong curricula, meaningful accountability and built-in protections. Legislatures can pass mandates, but the infrastructure needed to carry them out, including time, training, resources, oversight and implementation guidance, does not always follow.

Other areas of education routinely build in safeguards when classroom activities present foreseeable risks. Science laboratories have safety procedures, student health records receive privacy protections, and online learning platforms undergo data-security reviews. Financial education should be held to the same standard.

States can start by requiring protected classroom versions of any financial, employment, tax, student aid, loan, banking, insurance or benefits documents used in instruction. Sensitive fields should be removed, redacted or blocked, and standards should explicitly prohibit students from entering real Social Security numbers, birthdates, home addresses, wage information, account details or other personal data except through a legally authorized and properly secured process.

Teachers should also receive basic privacy and compliance training before leading activities involving financial forms. Schools need clear procedures for collecting, grading, storing, transmitting, retaining and destroying assignments that could contain personal information. Any third-party platform used for financial education should undergo an appropriate privacy and security review before students are asked to use it.

State departments of education should examine their existing standards and issue prompt clarification wherever protections are missing or ambiguous. Policymakers who supported financial literacy legislation should likewise determine whether the language they advanced may have unintentionally created opportunities for student data to be exposed.

None of this is an argument against financial education. It is an argument for delivering it responsibly.

Students should learn about taxes, employment forms, banking, credit, financial aid, loans, insurance and long-term planning. They should understand what financial documents ask, why the information matters and how different choices can affect their lives. Practical instruction, however, does not require real student data. Redacted forms that block students from entering personal information can still provide meaningful experience without compromising anyone’s privacy.

Some may argue that teachers can simply instruct students to use fictitious information. That is not an adequate policy. If a standard directs students to “complete a W-4” or “complete a loan application” without requiring a protected classroom version, safety depends on every educator independently identifying and correcting the problem. Sound policy should build in protection by design rather than leave it to individual judgment in thousands of classrooms.

Others may dismiss the concern because the documents are used only as classroom exercises. An assignment involving minors and sensitive personal information does not become harmless simply because it is educational. Whenever Social Security numbers, birthdates, addresses or family finances could be disclosed, privacy safeguards must be part of the lesson’s design.

The National Financial Educators Council has begun contacting federal agencies, state departments of education, attorneys general, state boards of education, policymakers and other relevant offices to request review and corrective action before the next school year. That timetable matters because every year these gaps remain, another group of students may face an entirely avoidable risk that may not surface until long after the assignment has been forgotten.

Financial education is too important to implement carelessly. If we expect students to become financially capable adults, we need instruction that is practical, rigorous, safe and professionally designed, with privacy protections, educator training and accountability built in from the start.

The principle is straightforward: Teach the concepts, explain the documents, prepare students for real financial decisions and protect the child.

No student should have to risk identity theft to learn financial literacy.

给狂奔的AI教育，画一条跑道——本周智能教育大事札记

这周智能教育领域最鲜明的信号，不是哪个产品又刷了屏，而是「规矩」这个词反复出现：中国发布了教育大模型团体标准，欧盟的 AI 透明度条款正式生效，越南把 AI 作弊写进了学术诚信规则，澳大利亚的学校则在犹豫要不要让 AI 进课堂。热闹了三年的 AI 教育，好像第一次集体意识到：光有技术还不够，还得想清楚怎么用、谁来用、什么能用。

一、北京这场大会，把「标准」二字摆上了台面

8 月 18 日至 20 日，2026 全球智慧教育大会在北京召开，北京师范大学与联合国教科文组织教育信息技术研究所联合主办，60 多个国家的代表围绕「人工智能与教育可持续发展」坐到了一起。大会期间，三项教育大模型团体标准正式发布：《通用教育大模型技术架构设计规范》《基于大模型的智慧教育体系建设导则》《智能教师技术规范》，从技术架构到体系建设再到「智能教师」，恰好覆盖了 AI 进教育的三条关键路径。

我的看法：这三项标准的意义，不在于它有多「硬」，而在于它把行业从「各说各话」拉到了「一张图纸上说话」。过去两年，各家教育大模型的能力评测、数据口径、安全规范五花八门，学校想选型都无从下手。标准解决的不是技术问题，是信任问题——就像高速公路刚通车时，最缺的不是好车，而是交通规则。和欧盟的审慎监管、美国的分散探索相比，中国这条路子更像是「先立规矩、再放行」，延续了它一贯的体系化偏好。

二、OpenAI 给 13 岁孩子做了个「青少年版」

8 月 18 日，OpenAI 发布青少年版 ChatGPT，面向 13 至 17 岁学生：新增「学习模式」，用引导性问题帮学生理解材料，而不是直接给答案；推出「负责任作业提醒」，在学生想抄近路时把他带回学习模式；家长控制、健康使用提醒、高风险内容干预也都配齐了。

我的看法：这件事最值得玩味的，是 OpenAI 把「少给答案」当成了卖点。前两年大家还在比谁答得又快又全，现在巨头开始比谁更会「忍住不给答案」——这是产品逻辑的一个分水岭。从用户视角看，这其实是被现实逼出来的：学校里防 AI 作弊防得风声鹤唳，厂商再不主动做「防滥用」，就要被学校和家长联手挡在门外。所以「青少年版」本质上是一次产品向教育规律的妥协，好在妥协的方向是对的。

三、作业帮的「AI 超级老师」，把学习机带进 5.0 时代

8 月 17 日，作业帮发布「AI 超级老师」，宣布学习机进入 5.0 时代：覆盖学情诊断、错因溯源、答疑讲解、作业辅导、个性化学习规划等全场景，诊断精度能细化到单道填空题，还同步推出两款高端旗舰和一个「口袋 AI 老师」。

我的看法：学习机这个品类很有意思，它是中国教育硬件最卷的战场。从点读机到平板再到「AI 老师」，每一次升级都是把「辅导班」往家里搬。但我的担心也在这里：当机器越来越会讲题，孩子会不会越来越不思考？诊断得再细，如果只是让刷题更高效，那 AI 就只是把「题海战术」提了速。厂商爱说「类人教师水准」，但我希望他们别忘了——好老师最重要的本事，不是讲得对，而是懂得什么时候不讲。

四、江苏、云南的 AI 课，这学期真的要开起来了

8 月 18 日，江苏省中小学人工智能通识教育课全覆盖项目正式启动，暑期系列培训累计收看 903 万人次；云南省也在 8 月中旬印发「人工智能+教育」行动方案，目标到 2030 年实现全学段覆盖，四年级、七年级每周 1 课时。

我的看法：政策从文件走向课表，这一步最难。江苏的做法里有一个细节很实在：针对乡村学校同步推出送培上门、云端课堂和技术专员帮扶，「确保每一所乡村学校都能开齐上好」。这比单纯发文有意义得多。AI 通识课最怕变成「城里孩子的特长班」，把乡村学校落在后面。而云南方案里那句「开发融入生物多样性、绿色能源等本土元素的特色课程」也让我会心一笑——AI 教育不应该是千篇一律的模板，它应该能装进每个地方的土里。

出勤是记分牌-连接是游戏

原文标题: Attendance is the scoreboard–connection is the game
来源: eSchoolNews | 发布时间: 2026-08-21

Key points:

  • Students go where they feel connected

  • Chronic absenteeism isn’t the problem–it’s the signal

  • Don’t wait for September: Reach students now, when it matters most

  • For more news on attendance, visit eSN’sEducational Leadershiphub

If I had been asked a few years ago, I would have told you that chronic absenteeism was a school system’s problem to fix. With better data systems, staff trained on outreach, and improved intervention protocols, school districts could move the numbers on their own. But today we can see that while the numbers moved a little, the deeper story didn’t change nearly as much as we hoped.

Why? Because chronic absenteeism isn’t the problem. It’s the symptom.

In speaking with school leaders across the country, I’ve found near-unanimous agreement that the real issue is disconnection. When a young person stops believing that school has anything to do with their future, they stop showing up. Not because they’re defiant. Not because they don’t care. Because somewhere along the way, school stopped feeling relevant to them.

Personally and professionally, the fact that some students don’t see school as relevant is hard to admit. It would be simpler to say that today’s kids and families need to try harder to get out of the house in the morning and go to school. But if you actually sit with a teenager who has disengaged from school and ask why, “I don’t feel like it” is rarely the answer. Rather, you’d hear something closer to “no one there really knows me” or “I don’t see the point.”

So we’ve shifted how we think about this work. Attendance is the outcome, not the mission. The mission is reconnecting young people to people, purpose, and opportunity. Get that right, and attendance follows. Chase attendance directly, without addressing connection, and you’re just measuring a symptom while the underlying condition festers.

Belonging beats compliance

Think about the places in your own life where you show up without anyone reminding you, like your gym or church or your job. You don’t need a policy to make you go to those places. You go because you feel you belong there.

Kids are no different. Students go where they feel connected. Belonging, trusted relationships, and hope for the future move young people far more reliably than rules ever will. You can install every attendance tracking system in the world, and it won’t out-motivate a kid’s need to feel like they matter to somebody.

This is where I think schools–districts like mine included–have sometimes gotten it backwards. We’ve treated disengagement, and by extension chronic absenteeism, as a compliance problem best solved with stricter policies and more consequences. But you can’t discipline your way into belonging. You have to create belonging.

Why schools can’t do this alone

Here’s the part that took me longer to accept: even the best schools can’t build that web of belonging by themselves.

I believe that the Ithaca City School District, which I am proud to lead, is home to extraordinary teachers, principals, counselors, and support staff. Each day, they pour themselves into our students, and I’ve seen them work near-miracles for individual students. But I believe in the old African adage, “It takes a village to raise a child.” No matter how gifted a teacher is, they can’t be the only trusted adult in a student’s life. Unfortunately, schools alone can’t create the community of relationships children need.

Humans are social creatures. Our brains are wired for connection and community, and that community has to extend beyond the school building.

That’s why I’ve stopped talking about youth disengagement as a school problem with a school solution. It’s a community issue that requires a community-wide answer. Reconnecting disconnected youth is not something schools do to communities. It’s something communities do with schools.

What this looks like in practice

Pause for a minute to consider all the parts of your community young people interact with after school. Each should ask: How can we become one more reason young people stay connected?

Youth sports. A coach who shows up early to talk with a struggling player about how things are at school before discussing the game plan is doing identity-building work, whether they know it or not.

Libraries. A librarian who remembers a teen’s name and saves the next book in a series is actually saying, “I know you, and I expect you back.”

Faith communities. Churches and congregations have long been anchors of belonging, offering young people mentors, structure, and a sense of purpose that stretches beyond any single Sunday service.

Employers. A local business owner who gives a 16-year-old their first job is handing that young person proof that the adult world has a place for them.

After-school providers. Here in Ithaca, we’re fortunate to have organizations like the Greater Ithaca Activities Center, the Ithaca Youth Bureau, and Village at Ithaca doing this work daily, wrapping kids in mentorship, activity, and community after the final bell rings.

Mental health agencies. Counselors and clinicians in the community help young people work through the barriers–trauma, anxiety, family stress–that no attendance letter home will ever resolve.

Mentoring organizations. A mentor who keeps a standing weekly appointment with a teenager teaches that young person, through simple consistency, that they’re worth showing up for.

Local businesses. In addition to offering jobs, businesses sponsoring teams, hosting events, or simply knowing their young neighbors by name, add another connection to the network.

None of these efforts are new. What’s new is naming them for what they are: essential infrastructure in the fight against youth disconnection, not just “nice to have” community programming on the side.

Building an ecosystem, not a campaign

We’re not simply trying to improve attendance numbers. We’re building a community ecosystem that reconnects young people to opportunity. That distinction matters more than it might sound.

An attendance campaign has a start date and an end date. It has a target and a deadline. An ecosystem doesn’t work that way. It grows, it adapts, and it depends on many different organisms doing their part, day after day, without any of them needing to control the whole thing.

Communities don’t solve chronic absenteeism by demanding students show up. They solve it by becoming places young people want to show up for. That’s a fundamentally different posture. It’s less about enforcement and more about invitation. Less about tracking kids down and more about giving them somewhere worth going.

A shared responsibility

I’m not suggesting we let schools off the hook for absenteeism, or that we should shift blame elsewhere. School systems still play the most prominent role in engaging students and ensuring students go to school, and we’re backing that up: building relational advisory structures, training staff in trauma-informed practice, redesigning what engagement looks like inside our buildings.

But we’ve also learned that our work only goes so far if it stops at the schoolhouse door. Reconnecting youth is a responsibility shared by schools, families, employers, youth organizations, places of worship, libraries, coaches, mentors, and local businesses. Each of those groups holds a piece of the puzzle that schools simply can’t hold alone.

Collective responsibility isn’t a slogan. It’s a practical necessity. If you want a young person to feel connected seven days a week, you need more than a five-day-a-week institution trying to do it single-handedly.

The real scoreboard

I keep coming back to this image: Attendance is the scoreboard, connection is the game. Watch any scoreboard by itself, and you’ll miss everything that actually matters, the strategy, the effort, the relationships built between teammates over a long season. The score just tells you the outcome. It doesn’t tell you how you got there.

The same is true for our young people. Attendance numbers tell us something happened. They don’t tell us why, and they certainly don’t tell us how to fix it. If we want different numbers, we need a different game–one built on belonging, trusted adults, and genuine opportunity.

So here’s my invitation to this community, and to every community wrestling with this same challenge: Don’t wait for the school district to solve youth disconnection on its own. It won’t, because it can’t. This is community development work as much as it is education work. If we want young people connected to school, to work, and to their own futures, every sector of our community has a role to play.

The question isn’t whether our kids will show up. The question is whether we’ve given them enough reasons to.
